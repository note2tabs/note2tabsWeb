Drum notation tells you which part of the kit to play and when to play it. Unlike a piano staff, its vertical positions usually describe kit voices rather than pitched notes. Start with the notation key for the score you are reading, then use the written rhythm to bring the parts together.

The [drum notation editor](/drum-notation-editor) lets you write a pattern and hear it back. Use a [blank drum track](/gte/new?track=drums) to try a simple groove while reading.

## Identify the kit voices

Look for the positions used for kick, snare, hi-hat and ride before reading a full bar. Drums commonly use ordinary noteheads, while cymbals commonly use crosses. The exact placement can vary between scores, so the legend or instrument labels matter more than memorising a position without context.

A cross notehead does not change the underlying rhythmic value. An eighth-note hi-hat still occupies half of a quarter-note beat. Its notehead identifies the sound; its stem and beam help you read the duration and grouping.

## Read the pulse and subdivision

Find the time signature and count a steady beat. In a simple 4/4 groove, count four quarter-note beats through each bar. Eighth-note hi-hats divide that count into “one-and, two-and, three-and, four-and.”

Start slowly enough to maintain the count. A sixteenth-note figure adds another subdivision, while a triplet divides a beat into three equal parts. Listen to the pattern and identify which subdivision you are following rather than treating every closely spaced note as the same rhythm.

## Read simultaneous hits together

A kick and a hi-hat can happen at exactly the same moment. The score may show different voices or stem directions to keep the parts readable. Those marks do not mean you should separate the attacks in time.

Try a basic backbeat: kick on beats one and three, snare on two and four, and eighth notes on the hi-hat. Follow the hi-hat count while identifying which main beats add a kick or snare. Then play the parts together in the editor.

## Treat rests as part of the groove

A rest can belong to one voice while another continues. If the hi-hat carries on through a gap in the kick pattern, the bar is not silent. Read each layer and then listen to the combined result.

The same principle helps with fills. A tom phrase may replace part of the main pattern while the feet maintain a pulse underneath it. Check which voices continue instead of assuming the whole kit changes together.

## Read across the end of the bar

A fill gains meaning from where it lands. Include the next downbeat when reading or practising the last bar of a section. Count through the transition into the following crash, kick or new groove.

Loop that transition at a comfortable playback speed. Increase the speed once the count remains steady and the kit voices are clear. Do not change the written rhythm just to make a practice session slower.

## Connect reading with writing

Enter a short pattern in the [drum editor](/gte/new?track=drums) and compare its notation with the sound. Change one hit and listen to what happens. This makes the symbols easier to remember than reading them without a musical reference.
