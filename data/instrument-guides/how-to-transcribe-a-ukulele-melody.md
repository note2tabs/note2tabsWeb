A ukulele recording and a ukulele tab answer different questions. The recording contains pitches, timing and articulation. The tab chooses strings and frets for a particular tuning. Work through those decisions in that order so a recognisable melody becomes a comfortable arrangement.

Start with [ukulele transcription](/ukulele-transcription) to review notes from a recording. Use the [ukulele tab editor](/ukulele-tab-editor) when you are ready to write the four-string part.

## Choose a melody you can follow

Select a short solo phrase or a fingerpicked passage where the melody is audible. Listen once before generating notes. Identify where the phrase starts, which note feels like its destination and where it repeats.

A recording of a ukulele on its own provides a straightforward reference. With accompaniment, follow the melodic line rather than assuming every detected pitch belongs to the part you want to play. Choose one phrase to review before moving through the song.

## Check pitches and rhythm separately

Listen to the generated notes and compare the line's direction with the recording. A step upward, a repeated note and a leap into another register each provide context when a single pitch is uncertain.

Then count the attacks. Does the melody begin on a beat, between beats or with a pickup into the next bar? Check note endings too. A held note can carry the phrase while an accompaniment continues underneath it.

## Choose the tuning for the tab

Standard ukulele tuning is G–C–E–A. High G uses G4 on the fourth string, while low G uses G3. The other open strings remain C4, E4 and A4. The choice changes which register is available on the fourth string.

Choose the preset that matches your instrument before entering the arrangement. A four-string tab drawn with the same fret numbers can sound different if the G string belongs to the other octave. The [high-G and low-G guide](/blog/how-to-write-ukulele-tabs-high-g-low-g) explains the relationship with examples.

## Choose positions for a playable phrase

Begin with the melody alone. Find positions that connect without unnecessary jumps, then try the complete phrase on the instrument. Some notes can be played on several strings; choose a sequence rather than judging every fret in isolation.

Use an open string when it helps the musical line or gives your hand time to shift. Use a fretted position when it connects more comfortably to the next notes. Playback checks the pitches and rhythm, while your hands help confirm the fingering.

## Add accompaniment after the melody works

Introduce the supporting notes one at a time. In high-G tuning, a note on the fourth string can sit within the melody's range rather than beneath it. Listen to the complete voicing instead of treating the lowest drawn line as the lowest sound.

Keep the main melody recognisable. A simpler accompaniment that you can play consistently is more useful than a dense arrangement that obscures the phrase you wanted to learn.

## Save the arrangement for practice

Keep the original transcription and the arranged part together in your project. Save the song to your library or export a native project file. A text tab provides readable fret positions; MIDI carries pitches and timing into another music application.
