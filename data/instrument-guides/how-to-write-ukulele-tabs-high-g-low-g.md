Ukulele tab is straightforward once the string order and tuning are clear. The four lines tell you which string to play, and the fret number tells you where. Rhythm and note length turn that sequence into a phrase you can actually practise.

Open the [ukulele tab editor](/ukulele-tab-editor) to try the examples in a four-string track. If the idea is still in a recording, start with [ukulele transcription](/ukulele-transcription) to review its notes and timing.

## Read the four lines in the right order

Standard ukulele tuning is G–C–E–A from the fourth string to the first. On the page, tab is read from top to bottom as **A, E, C, G**. The top line represents the first string, not the lowest-sounding note.

A zero means an open string. A three on the top A line means the third fret of that string, which gives C5 in standard tuning. A zero on the C line gives C4. These two notes share a name but sound an octave apart.

Write a short melody on one or two strings before arranging a full song. It is easier to check the fret numbers and rhythm when the phrase is small.

## Choose high G or low G before arranging

High-G tuning uses G4–C4–E4–A4. The fourth string is higher than the C and E strings, so moving downward through the drawn tab does not always move downward in pitch. This is called re-entrant tuning.

Low-G tuning changes the fourth string to G3. The other open strings stay C4, E4 and A4. A melody or accompaniment that uses the fourth string can therefore sound an octave different even when the written fret numbers are identical.

Choose the preset that matches your instrument. Listen to a phrase that uses the G string and confirm the register before writing the rest of the arrangement.

## Keep pitch and fingering separate

A note is a pitch; a tab position is a way of playing it. Some pitches have several available positions. Choose a position that connects comfortably to the notes before and after it.

For example, a phrase may be easier if it stays near one hand position, even if another version uses fewer string changes. Try the complete phrase slowly rather than judging a single fret in isolation.

When arranging notes from a recording, review the pitches first. Then place them on the four-string track for your tuning. Automatic pitch detection does not decide which ukulele fingering suits your hand or arrangement.

## Write the rhythm, not just the numbers

Set each note's start and length. A four-note phrase can sound completely different when one note is held and the next two are short. Leave space for rests rather than filling every gap with another note.

Play the bar back and count the pulse. If the phrase begins before beat one, keep that pickup in mind when deciding where to start the next bar. Repeat the phrase only after the rhythm is clear.

## Build the fingerpicked arrangement

Add accompaniment notes beneath the melody one at a time. Check which notes happen together and which alternate. In high G, a G-string note may sit inside the melody's range rather than below it, so listen to the whole voicing.

Keep the original melody recognisable while you add the supporting part. A comfortable arrangement is one you can play through, not simply the one with the most notes.

## Save and share the part

Keep a native project file to preserve the tuning and editor data. Export a text tab for a readable four-string part, or MIDI when another music tool needs the pitches and timing. Sign in to keep the arrangement in your library.
