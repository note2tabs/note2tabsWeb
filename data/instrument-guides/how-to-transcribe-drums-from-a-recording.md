A drum transcription is a map of events across a kit. Several sounds can happen at once, and the difference between a groove and a fill is often how those events connect to the next bar. Check the rhythm in layers instead of trying to read the whole kit at once.

Start with [drum transcription](/drum-transcription) for a recording, or open a [blank drum track](/gte/new?track=drums) if you already know the pattern.

## Find the pulse before the fill

Choose a short passage with a clear beat. Listen through it and count a steady pulse before generating or editing the notes. A drum stem, rehearsal take or clear drum cover gives you a useful reference for comparison.

Identify the start of the bar. A crash can mark the beginning of a section, but not every crash is a downbeat. Listen to the repeated kick and snare pattern over several bars to establish where the groove resolves.

## Check the kick pattern

Follow the bass drum first. Mark the attacks you hear and compare their spacing with the transcription. In a simple rock beat, a kick on beats one and three provides an easy reference, but an extra hit before a snare can change the groove substantially.

Do not judge a kick from the visual size of an audio waveform alone. Bass guitar and other low sounds can overlap it. Listen to the attack and use the generated drum track as an editable starting point.

## Add the snare and cymbal subdivision

Listen for the snare backbeat next. A snare and hi-hat may happen on exactly the same subdivision; they should remain separate kit events at the same time, not be moved apart to make the page look less crowded.

Then follow the hi-hat or ride. Is the pulse divided into eighths, sixteenths or a triplet feel? Count it aloud while listening. This subdivision is the reference for checking a quiet extra snare note or a kick that falls between the main beats.

Drum notation commonly uses different noteheads for drums and cymbals. Read each position with its kit voice rather than interpreting it as a pitched melody. In the editor, use the kit lanes to inspect which sound belongs to each hit.

## Check a fill together with its landing

Select the fill and the first beat after it. Listen across the transition: a run of toms can be correct in isolation yet leave too much space before the following crash, or overlap the next bar unexpectedly.

Keep the beat count running while checking the fill. If you cannot count it at the song's speed, slow practice playback and follow the subdivision. Adjust timing only after you know which part of the beat each hit belongs to.

## Listen to the complete groove

Bring the layers back together. A correct list of kick, snare and cymbal hits still needs to feel like a single pattern. Play the phrase through twice before changing it again. Check repeated bars for accidental extra hits and compare the transition back into the loop.

Keep bass or other tracks in the project when you want to hear the rhythm section together. Use the drum part alone when you need to focus on a specific hit.

## Export for practice or production

Keep the project for practice and future editing. Export MIDI when you want to arrange the drum events in a DAW and choose your own kit sound. Check the destination software's drum mapping so the imported pitches trigger the intended kit pieces.
